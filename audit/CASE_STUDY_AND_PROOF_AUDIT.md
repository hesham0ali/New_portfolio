# Case Study and Proof Audit

## Overall finding

The repository has three project pages, but they function primarily as expanded portfolio entries rather than decision-grade case studies. Sho9 is the strongest proof for the current Salla positioning, yet it lacks the visual, narrative, and outcome evidence needed to reduce buyer uncertainty. The other two demonstrate technical breadth but include little verifiable proof and do not directly reinforce the primary offer.

No recommendation below authorizes inventing client results, metrics, testimonials, dates, or implementation details.

## Sho9 assessment

### Confirmed strengths

- It is a real, named Salla store and includes a live external link.
- It states Hesham's role and ongoing maintenance relationship.
- It is prominently featured on the homepage.
- It aligns directly with the target audience and service positioning.

### Missing proof — P1

- The business need or initial problem.
- The exact scope completed by Hesham.
- What came from standard Salla capabilities versus custom work.
- Important design or technical constraints.
- Decisions made and why.
- Client-approved screenshots or annotated interface details.
- A dated timeline or project stage, if it can be verified.
- Documented outcomes, whether quantitative or qualitative.

### Recommended case-study structure

1. **Context:** What the store sells, its audience, and the state of the project when Hesham became involved.
2. **Need:** The concrete business or storefront problem.
3. **Role and scope:** Precisely what Hesham owned and what was outside scope.
4. **Constraints:** Platform limitations, content readiness, integrations, launch requirements, or maintenance conditions.
5. **Work completed:** A factual list of approved tasks.
6. **Key decisions:** Why specific storefront, theme, navigation, mobile, or technical choices were made.
7. **Evidence:** Approved before/after images, final screenshots, annotated details, and live links.
8. **Outcome:** Only verified changes or client-approved observations. If no metric exists, describe the delivered state without implying uplift.
9. **Ongoing work:** What maintenance means in practice, if disclosure is approved.
10. **Related service and CTA:** Link to the relevant service page and explain the next step.

### Visual proof requirements

- Use original or client-approved screenshots, not stock mockups presented as the project.
- Label images by device, page, feature, and date where useful.
- Add specific alt text describing what the screenshot proves.
- Avoid before/after comparisons unless both states are authentic and permission is clear.
- Avoid decorative galleries that add no evidence.

## Trust hierarchy

The site should prioritize proof in this order:

1. **First-party artifacts:** real screenshots, live work, code or architecture excerpts that can be shared.
2. **Attributed client evidence:** an approved quotation with name/role/company, or a client-owned page that confirms the relationship.
3. **Verified operational facts:** dates, scope, platform, role, and clearly defined volumes.
4. **Documented outcomes:** measured or client-confirmed changes with method and timeframe.
5. **Self-description:** useful context, but weaker when not supported by the above.

## Claims policy

- Never infer revenue, conversion, speed, ranking, or sales impact from a completed redesign.
- Never turn platform-wide user counts into personal results.
- Do not use “increased,” “improved,” or “optimized” without stating what changed and how it was assessed.
- If exact numbers are confidential, use an approved bounded description rather than a fabricated or rounded metric.
- Record the source and approval status of each material claim in project content data or an internal evidence log.
- Keep structured data consistent with visible, verified copy.
